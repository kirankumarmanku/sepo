\section{SFT Dataset Details}
\label{app:data}
The SFT corpus contains approximately 44,000 chain-of-thought episodes across three datasets: \textit{sepo\_sft\_data\_multi} (IPD + Auction + Negotiation v1, ${\sim}25{,}590$ train / 6,398 valid) and game-specific datasets for Kuhn Poker (${\sim}8{,}000$ examples) and GTBench negotiation (${\sim}4{,}000$ examples). Each example is a chat-format JSONL record with a full game history and action token; chain-of-thought reasoning is included in the assistant turn. Action tokens per game: \texttt{COOPERATE}/\texttt{DEFECT} (IPD), \texttt{LOW}/\texttt{MEDIUM}/\texttt{HIGH} (Auction), integer demands (Negotiation), \texttt{BET}/\texttt{PASS}/\texttt{CALL}/\texttt{FOLD} (Kuhn Poker).
Table~\ref{tab:sft_weights} shows the strategy mixture weights used to generate episodes. Weights reflect the SEPO-optimal strategy distribution rather than a uniform draw, biasing the SFT model toward equilibrium-aligned behavior from the start.

\section{Original-Pipeline Control Arms}
\label{app:ablation}
This appendix reports the re-evaluation, under the corrected stochastic protocol, of checkpoints trained by the original (defective) pipeline and by the intermediate per-rollout-auxiliary variant---the reward-only arms of the mechanism study (main \S6).
\paragraph{IPD arms (Gemma 4), best checkpoints.} Per-rollout auxiliary arm: exploit $0.313$, safety $+1.741$ (s50). Credited arm trained with a defective action-extraction sensor: exploit $0.313$, safety $+1.752$ (final). Corrected \sepo{}: exploit $0.313$, safety $+1.648$ (s25). As discussed in main \S6, best-checkpoint exploit and safety are similar across arms---the payoff signal alone suffices for retaliation learning in IPD---but the arms differ sharply in trajectory: the per-rollout arm's training-time collusion rises to $1.0$ (unconditional defection) while the corrected arm's falls to $0.25$ before the normalization-driven collapse; only the corrected arm has a live gradient opposing trusting-partner exploitation.
\paragraph{Kuhn Poker arms (Qwen), full trajectories.} The original-pipeline checkpoints execute a random walk under identical evaluation: exploit $0.409$ (s25), $0.520$ (s50), $0.289$ (s75), $0.501$ (final), with card-conditioning never emerging (bet rates for J/Q/K statistically indistinguishable at every checkpoint) and dominated actions drifting (call-K falls to $0.02$ at s50). The originally-reported ``zero exploit-pool advantage at the best checkpoint'' corresponds to selecting a favorable point on this walk. Contrast the corrected pipeline's monotone Gemma trajectory (main Table~5).
\paragraph{Per-round normalization.} The per-round (vs.\ episode-level) advantage normalization of Eq.~4 remains necessary independently of penalty crediting: the SFT prior is near-deterministic, so episode-level outcomes frequently coincide across rollouts ($\sigma \approx 0$), while per-step stochasticity keeps $\sigma_t$ non-zero. Our original claim that per-rollout \emph{penalty} computation breaks the constant-baseline cancellation was incorrect---an auxiliary-sampled penalty varies across the group but is independent of the credited actions, contributing variance rather than signal (main \S3.3); crediting is what breaks the cancellation.

\subsection{Forensic Timeline and Log Signatures}
\label{app:forensics}
The gradient-cancellation defect (main \S6) has the following documented history, reconstructible from our released repository. \textbf{May 11}: the per-rollout fix landed on the development branch (commit ``Fix GRPO reward: per-rollout exploit episodes so SEPO penalty varies in advantage''). \textbf{May 15}: a merge-conflict resolution on the working branch, bundled inside an unrelated data-generation commit, resolved the training file back to the shared-penalty version (the pre-resolution state was preserved as a \texttt{.bak} file in the same commit, which is what makes this forensically checkable). \textbf{May 17--23}: all originally-reported GRPO runs executed on the reverted file.
Which code path a run executed is identifiable post hoc from its training log. The shared/cached path writes step records in which exploitability, collusion, and externality are all exactly $0.000$ (the cache skips their computation); these appear on 11--65\% of steps across the six original runs, on the cache's refresh schedule (steps $1$--$4$, $6$--$9$, \ldots between refreshes at multiples of 5). The corrected pipeline computes per-episode penalties every step and cannot emit this signature; it additionally logs each rollout's penalty $P_r$ explicitly, so a group-constant penalty---the failure mode---is visible in the first minutes of any run. Two further defects shared the same provenance and are documented in the released diffs: rollout prompts were generated without the assistant-turn marker (training on a format evaluation never sees), and the generation stopping criteria fired on incomplete lines, truncating chain-of-thought at the first action-word mention and parsing reasoning fragments as decisions (e.g., the fragment ``No defection'' recorded as DEFECT on traces that conclude COOPERATE; 10/10 reproduction on held prompts).
\section{Game Descriptions and Payoff Structures}
\label{app:games}
\paragraph{IPD.} 8-round iterated prisoner's dilemma. Actions: COOPERATE / DEFECT. Payoff matrix: mutual cooperation $(3,3)$, mutual defection $(1,1)$, unilateral defect $(5,0)$. Subgame-perfect equilibrium of the finite game is mutual defection; in practice LLMs exhibit bounded rationality and TFT is the cooperative benchmark.\footnote{We treat TFT convergence as the behavioral equilibrium following the behavioral economics literature.} GrimTrigger cooperates until any defection, then defects permanently.
\paragraph{Auction.} 6-round sealed-bid auction with private values drawn from $\{2, 4\}$ uniformly. Bids: LOW=1, MEDIUM=2, HIGH=3. Winner (higher bid) earns value$-$bid; loser earns 0; ties split. AggressiveBid always bids HIGH. No Nash equilibrium exists against AggressiveBid (exploit rises monotonically after step 25).
\paragraph{Negotiation v1.} 4-round single-issue bargaining (pie = 10, complete information). Simultaneous integer demands; deal if combined $\leq 10$, both earn demand. Breakdown: both earn 0. SelfishSplit demands 7; HardballSplit demands 8. Structural ceiling: the base model approximates the optimal counter-strategy ($\text{demand} = 10 - \text{opp\_demand}$) directly from the prompt.
\paragraph{Negotiation v2 (GTBench).} 4-round multi-issue bargaining. Three items (Books, Hats, Balls) with random pool sizes $[1{-}4]$ per item per episode. Private integer valuations per player sum to 10. Deal if demands $\leq$ pool on all items; payoff $= \sum_i \text{demand}_i \times \text{value}_i$. GreedyNeg demands maximum of all items; HardballNeg demands maximum of its two highest-value items.
\paragraph{Kuhn Poker.} 6-hand episodes; deck: Jack (J), Queen (Q), King (K). One card dealt per player, 1-chip ante. Betting sequence: player 1 PASS or BET; player 2 responds. Showdown: higher card wins pot. Nash equilibrium for the first player: King always bets; Jack bluff-bets with probability $\frac{1}{3}$; Queen calls with probability $\frac{2}{3}$ (at the $\alpha{=}1/3$ endpoint of the one-parameter NE family).
\section{Negotiation Game Variants}
\label{app:negotiation-variants}
We use two distinct negotiation variants: \textbf{v1} (single-issue, complete-information) and \textbf{v2} (multi-issue, private-valuation, GTBench-style). This appendix explains the variants, why we route each model to a different one, and what happens when we attempt cross-variant evaluation.
\paragraph{v1 (single-issue, complete information).} Two agents repeatedly bargain over a divisible pie of size~10. Each round, both submit an integer demand $a \in \{1,\dots,9\}$. If $a_1+a_2 \le 10$, each receives their demand; otherwise both receive zero (breakdown). 4 rounds per episode. Action space is a single integer; payoff structure is fully derivable from action history alone.
\paragraph{v2 (multi-issue, GTBench).} Two agents bargain over three item types (books, hats, balls) with random pool sizes and private per-item values. Each round, agents submit a demand vector $[a, b, c]$. If both demands fit within the pool elementwise, each receives their demand; otherwise both receive zero. Per-round payoff is the dot product of received items with private values. 4 rounds per episode. Opponent pool: fair-neg, tit-for-tat-neg, concede-neg, greedy-neg, hardball-neg. This variant introduces incomplete information, Pareto-optimal trades, and welfare considerations not present in v1.
\subsection{Constrained-Fallback-Decode Rates}
When free-form generation contains no parseable action, the constrained decode (main \S3.5) supplies one. Because this changes what behavior is being measured, we report per-condition fallback counts for the Kuhn Poker evaluations (16 episodes $\times$ 6 opponents, ${\approx}550$ decisions each): Qwen base 135 (${\sim}25\%$ of decisions), Qwen SFT 39, Qwen original-pipeline checkpoints 45--60, Gemma~4 conditions single-digit counts per evaluation. The high base-Qwen rate means a quarter of its measured actions are argmax-over-action-tokens rather than sampled free-form commitments; conclusions about base Qwen's strategy carry this caveat. All corrected-pipeline evaluations use \texttt{max\_new\_tokens} $= 512$, matching training.

\subsection{Implications}
Under the corrected pipeline we treat variant choice as a property of the study design rather than a per-model routing decision: the main paper reports Gemma~4 on v2 (the variant requiring genuine multi-round inference) and drops per-model routing, which the reviewer-facing concern about post-hoc selection makes untenable. The broader observation that survives is about SFT adequacy (main \S5, Kuhn): a model's ability to benefit from \sepo{} on a variant is gated by whether its SFT installs that variant's basic structure, not by the variant's headroom alone.
