\begin{abstract}
Language models fine-tuned with reinforcement learning typically optimize for task reward, ignoring multi-agent strategic structure. We propose Safe Equilibrium Policy Optimization (\sepo{}), a training objective that augments expected payoff with explicit penalties for exploitability, collusion risk, and externality cost, implemented over Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Our central implementation finding is that the objective trains only if each penalty is credited to the episodes that produce it: a penalty shared across a rollout group cancels exactly under advantage normalization, and a penalty estimated from auxiliary episodes has zero expected gradient---both reduce \sepo{} to reward-only RL, a failure we demonstrate with a four-arm mechanism study (including our own originally-reported runs, which a silently-reverted fix caused to train reward-only; we document the forensics and release all logs). With credited per-pool penalties and a stochastic evaluation protocol---greedy decoding cannot express the mixed strategies that equilibrium play requires---Gemma~4 E4B-it's Kuhn Poker bluff rate converges monotonically onto the Nash mixture ($0.06 \to 0.37$ against the analytic $1/3$), and on Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma \sepo{} cuts exploitability 27\% below SFT at unchanged payoff and zero collusion. We further identify a general hazard for multi-objective GRPO (group normalization erases inter-pool $\lambda$ weighting, driving late-training collapse), a domain-dependent SFT exploit regression that \sepo{} partially corrects, and an SFT-quality precondition for equilibrium learning (Qwen~3.5-4B). We release our code (\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/sepo-2668}), SFT datasets, and training logs for both pipelines.
\end{abstract}

\label{sec:intro}

Two LLM-powered procurement agents, each serving a different organization, interact repeatedly on a digital marketplace. Without explicit coordination, they converge on a price-splitting pattern that benefits both principals but inflates costs for downstream consumers. No agent was programmed to collude; each maximized its own reward in a repeated game. This is algorithmic collusion: emergent, hard to detect, and a direct consequence of reward maximization in a strategic setting. As language agents take on higher-stakes roles in negotiation, resource management, automated trading, and multi-party planning, such failures become systemic risks rather than theoretical curiosities \citep{bakhtin2022human,park2023generative,fu2023improving}.
Standard alignment methods address this problem at the wrong level. RLHF \citep{christiano2017deep,ouyang2022training} and Constitutional AI \citep{bai2022constitutional} align a single agent to a fixed preference model, but in multi-agent environments the effective reward function depends on what other agents do. An agent trained to be helpful and harmless in isolation can still learn to exploit weaker opponents, sustain collusive equilibria with cooperative partners, or impose external costs on the shared system. These are not alignment failures in the conventional sense; they are failures of objective design for strategic settings.
We propose Safe Equilibrium Policy Optimization (\sepo{}), a training framework that addresses this gap by augmenting task utility with three penalty terms:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sepo}
\Jsepo(\pi) = u(\pi) - \lambda_e \cdot e(\pi) - \lambda_c \cdot c(\pi) - \lambda_x \cdot x(\pi)
\end{equation}
where $u(\pi)$ is expected payoff, $e(\pi)$ is an exploitability proxy estimated against adversarial opponents, $c(\pi)$ is collusion risk estimated against cooperative partners, and $x(\pi)$ is domain-specific externality cost. Each term targets a distinct strategic failure mode; the $\lambda$ coefficients are tuned per-domain.
\sepo{} is implemented as a reward signal applied to language models that generate game actions. Training proceeds in two stages: a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) warm-start on expert strategy traces, followed by \sepo{} optimization computed from fresh opponent interactions at every rollout. We evaluate on Gemma 4 E4B-it and Qwen 3.5-4B across five strategic domains: Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma (IPD), repeated sealed-bid auctions, single-issue negotiation, GTBench multi-issue negotiation \citep{wu2024gtbench}, and Kuhn Poker.
We report four main findings. First, under \sepo{} with credited penalties, Gemma~4's Kuhn Poker policy converges onto the Nash mixed strategy, measured directly by card-conditioned action frequencies: the bluff rate rises monotonically across checkpoints from 0.06 to 0.37 against the analytic $1/3$, with value-betting and never-bet-Queen behavior pinned at their equilibrium values. Second, the penalty's gradient path is the critical implementation choice: shared penalties cancel exactly, auxiliary-credited penalties have zero expected gradient, and only per-pool crediting trains the objective---a claim we verify with a four-arm study whose reward-only arms include our own originally-reported runs. Third, group-wise advantage normalization erases the $\lambda$ weighting \emph{between} penalty pools, producing a characteristic late-training collapse toward defection that we analyze and manage with objective-based checkpoint selection. Fourth, the SFT exploit regression is domain-dependent (a $17\times$ regression on multi-issue negotiation; none on IPD under stochastic evaluation), and SFT quality is a precondition for equilibrium learning: where SFT fails to install a game's basic structure, RL does not recover it at 4B scale.
Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A training objective for strategic language agents that jointly penalizes exploitability, collusion risk, and externality cost, with a per-pool credit-assignment implementation whose necessity we establish analytically and empirically.
\item A mechanism study isolating the objective's gradient path across four arms, with released training logs for both the corrected and the original (reward-only) pipelines---including the forensic account of how the original pipeline came to train a different objective than designed.
\item An empirical study across five strategic domains under a stochastic evaluation protocol suited to mixed strategies, identifying the boundary conditions---learnable equilibrium, adequate SFT, and pre-collapse stopping---under which equilibrium convergence occurs.
\end{enumerate}
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\subsection{Language Agents in Strategic Environments}
Language models have been deployed as autonomous agents in strategic domains. \citet{bakhtin2022human} demonstrated human-level Diplomacy play by combining a language model with game-theoretic planning at inference time. \citet{park2023generative} showed that LLM-powered agents produce emergent social behaviors (cooperation, coalition formation, information diffusion) when placed in persistent multi-agent environments. These systems optimize for task completion rather than strategic safety properties of the resulting equilibrium.
Several lines of work study LLMs as players in well-defined games. \citet{fu2023improving} applied self-play with AI feedback to improve LLM negotiation, gaining deal rates and individual payoff. \citet{guo2023suspicion} evaluated GPT-4 with theory-of-mind prompting in imperfect-information games. \citet{mao2025alympics} benchmarked LLMs in classic game-theoretic scenarios, revealing systematic deviations from Nash equilibrium. \citet{zhang2025klevel} proposed $K$-level reasoning to induce higher-order strategic beliefs, improving performance in matrix games. \citet{du2024improving} showed that multi-agent debate improves factual accuracy.
Foundational NLP work on negotiation predates the current wave of LLM agents. \citet{lewis2017deal} introduced Deal-or-No-Deal and found that reward-maximizing agents developed deceptive strategies. \citet{he2018decoupling} separated strategic planning from language generation for more controllable behavior. \citet{xu2023exploring} studied LLMs in Werewolf, finding that strategic communication emerges from in-context reasoning. These studies collectively establish that objective design shapes strategic behavior in language agents, the central premise of \sepo{}.
\subsection{Multi-Agent RL and Social Dilemmas}
\citet{leibo2017multiagent} introduced sequential social dilemmas, showing that independent RL agents converge to defection even when mutual cooperation yields higher welfare. \citet{lerer2017maintaining} showed that deep RL can maintain cooperation through opponent history conditioning. \citet{foerster2018learning} proposed LOLA, which differentiates through an opponent's anticipated learning step to shape its future policy toward cooperation.
Social preference engineering has been used to modify reward signals. \citet{jaques2019social} used social influence as intrinsic motivation; \citet{hughes2018inequity} embedded inequity aversion into reward functions. \citet{zhang2024scalable} addressed safe MARL with constrained optimization over joint behavior.
\sepo{} shares the goal of safer multi-agent outcomes but decomposes it differently. Where social influence and inequity aversion modify the reward to encourage specific prosocial behaviors, \sepo{} penalizes three failure modes as separate terms. This decomposition supports ablation and per-domain diagnosis.
\subsection{Equilibrium Training and Exploitability Minimization}
Computing or approximating equilibria has driven strategic AI from Go \citep{silver2016mastering} to multiplayer poker \citep{brown2019superhuman}. \citet{brown2019deep} scaled counterfactual regret minimization with neural networks. \citet{heinrich2016deep} proposed Neural Fictitious Self-Play (NFSP) for approximate Nash equilibria in two-player games. \citet{lanctot2017unified} introduced PSRO, iteratively expanding a strategy portfolio via best-response computation. \citet{lockhart2019computing} proposed exploitability descent as a direct gradient-based approach to Nash equilibria. These methods target exploitability alone. \sepo{} incorporates exploitability minimization as one component ($e(\pi)$) and adds collusion risk ($c(\pi)$) and externality cost ($x(\pi)$). A Nash equilibrium can be socially harmful (a cartel is stable but collusive), so equilibrium training alone does not address the full range of strategic safety concerns.
\subsection{Safe and Value-Aligned Optimization}
\citet{christiano2017deep} introduced RLHF by learning reward models from human preferences; \citet{ouyang2022training} scaled this to InstructGPT. \citet{bai2022constitutional} replaced human feedback with AI-generated critiques guided by principles. \citet{rafailov2023direct} eliminated the explicit reward model via direct preference optimization. \citet{achiam2017constrained} introduced CPO for safe single-agent RL with cost constraints. \citet{amodei2016concrete} taxonomized AI safety problems including reward hacking and side effects that intensify in multi-agent settings.
All of these methods assume a single agent in a fixed environment. \sepo{} fills the gap: its penalty terms are computed against other agents' strategies, making safety constraints inherently multi-agent in scope.
\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:problem}
Each domain is a finite two-player repeated game with $T$ rounds and a discrete action vocabulary $\mathcal{A}$ (Appendix~F). At round $t$ the agent observes a natural-language description $s_t$ of the game state and interaction history and emits an action $a_t \in \mathcal{A}$ through free-form generation followed by parsing (\S\ref{sec:parse}); a policy $\pi$ is the language model defining $\pi(a_t \mid s_t)$. Opponents are drawn from three fixed strategy pools: training $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{tr}}$, exploit $\mathcal{E}$, and collusive $\mathcal{C}$ (\S\ref{sec:grpo}). For an opponent $o$, let $\bar{u}_\pi^{(o)}$ and $\bar{u}_o$ denote the mean per-round payoffs of the agent and of $o$ over episodes between them. Every term of the objective is a pool expectation of an episode statistic: $u(\pi) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{tr}}|}\sum_{o \in \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{tr}}} \bar{u}_\pi^{(o)}$, and $e(\pi)$, $c(\pi)$, $x(\pi)$ are defined over $\mathcal{E}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ below.
\subsection{The \sepo{} Objective}
\label{sec:objective}
The \sepo{} objective is Equation~\ref{eq:sepo}, repeated here with formal definitions of each term.
\paragraph{Expected payoff $u(\pi)$.} The agent's mean reward against a pool of cooperative training opponents drawn from a domain-specific strategy library. This term recovers the standard task reward objective when used alone.
\paragraph{Exploitability $e(\pi)$.} The mean payoff advantage that adversarial opponents achieve over the agent. We estimate
\begin{equation*}
e(\pi) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}|}\sum_{o \in \mathcal{E}} \max\!\bigl(0,\; \bar{u}_o - \bar{u}_\pi^{(o)}\bigr),
\end{equation*}
where $\mathcal{E}$ is the exploit pool of adversarial strategy types, $\bar{u}_o$ is opponent $o$'s mean payoff in episodes against the agent, and $\bar{u}_\pi^{(o)}$ is the agent's mean payoff in those same episodes; the clipping at zero ensures the term penalizes being exploited without rewarding the agent for beating adversaries. This estimates exploit-pool advantage rather than true worst-case exploitability. Minimizing $e(\pi)$ pushes the policy toward best-response stability: a policy with $e(\pi) = 0$ cannot be profitably exploited by any opponent in the pool. This approximates Nash equilibrium when the exploit pool spans the adversarial strategy space.
\paragraph{Collusion risk $c(\pi)$.} The rate at which the agent engages in harmful coordination when paired with a collusive partner. We estimate $c(\pi)$ by measuring joint behavior with collusive partner types (e.g., partners that reciprocate high extraction in resource games, or partners that bid low in auctions). Penalizing $c(\pi)$ discourages correlated equilibria that are jointly profitable but socially harmful.
\paragraph{Externality cost $x(\pi)$.} Domain-specific harm imposed outside the bilateral interaction: welfare shortfall from the social optimum in social dilemmas, depletion in resource games, allocative inefficiency in auctions, and breakdown rate in negotiation. Minimizing $x(\pi)$ internalizes external costs that the private payoff function ignores.
\paragraph{Connection to game-theoretic concepts.} The $u(\pi)$ term alone maximizes individual payoff with no equilibrium incentive. Adding $e(\pi)$ drives the policy toward Nash best-response stability. Penalizing $c(\pi)$ discourages privately profitable correlated equilibria that harm the system. Penalizing $x(\pi)$ provides a Pigouvian correction at the policy level. Together, the three terms constitute an objective that simultaneously rewards individual competence and penalizes the three principal modes of strategic failure.
\subsection{\sepo{} Implementation}
\label{sec:grpo}
\sepo{} is trained using Group Relative Policy Optimization \citep[GRPO;][]{shao2024deepseekmath} as the underlying RL algorithm. Throughout the rest of this paper we refer to the full training procedure (GRPO + the \sepo{} objective) simply as \sepo{}. Payoff-denominated terms are first mapped to a common scale via $s = 3.0/\text{game.max\_payoff}$, anchoring every game to the standard IPD mutual-cooperation payoff of 3.0; this makes the $\lambda$ coefficients transferable across games with different payoff magnitudes. The utility and exploitability terms are scaled by $s$; collusion and externality are already normalized rates in $[0,1]$ and are left unscaled.
\paragraph{Per-pool credit assignment.} The central implementation requirement is that each term of $\Jsepo$ be differentiated through the episodes that \emph{produce} it. $\Jsepo$ is a sum of pool expectations, so its policy gradient decomposes into one policy-gradient term per pool. We therefore treat every (pool, opponent) pair---training, exploiter, and collusive alike---as a GRPO rollout group of $n$ episodes, and assign each episode a reward equal to its own contribution to $\Jsepo$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r_r = s \cdot u_r \cdot \mathbb{1}[\text{pool} = \text{train}] \;-\; \underbrace{\lambda_e s\, e_r + \lambda_c c_r + \lambda_x x_r}_{P_r}
\end{equation}
where $u_r$, $e_r$, $c_r$, $x_r$ are computed from episode $r$ alone: a training-pool episode carries its payoff and externality, an exploiter-pool episode carries its exploitability and externality, and a collusive-pool episode carries its collusion rate. Two seemingly-natural alternatives provably contribute \emph{no} gradient from the penalty terms, and \S\ref{sec:mechanism} demonstrates both failures empirically. (i)~A penalty estimate \emph{shared} across the rollout group is a group constant, which cancels identically under advantage normalization (the constant control-variate property; \citealp{williams1992simple,kool2019buy}). (ii)~A penalty computed from separate auxiliary episodes but credited to the training episode's log-probabilities is, conditional on the policy, \emph{independent} of the credited actions: $\mathbb{E}[P_r \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a^{\mathrm{train}}_r)] = \mathbb{E}[P_r] \cdot \mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a^{\mathrm{train}}_r)] = 0$, so per-rollout auxiliary sampling converts the exact cancellation into pure variance. Only crediting each penalty to the actions that caused it yields a non-degenerate gradient for $\lambda_e$, $\lambda_c$, $\lambda_x$.
\paragraph{Opponent pool design.} Three pools serve separated \emph{roles}: (1)~\textit{Training pool}: diverse cooperative strategies that generate the utility signal $u_r$; (2)~\textit{Exploit pool}: adversarial strategies that punish passive compliance (e.g., AlwaysDefect, AggressiveBid), used to estimate $e(\pi)$; (3)~\textit{Collusive pool}: cooperative partners that reveal greedy tendencies, used to estimate $c(\pi)$. The pools are separated by role rather than strictly disjoint: a benign strategy can serve both as a training opponent and as a measurement probe (e.g., FairNeg for collusion), since the two roles measure different quantities, and the primary exploiter is excluded from the training pool in every game except Negotiation v1, where the hardball strategy contributes to both signals (pool listing in Appendix~H). What matters is role consistency: an early implementation assigned the primary exploiter to \emph{all three} pools, creating conflicting gradient signals for identical actions and nullifying the safety gradient. Because the exploit pool is fixed during training, reported exploit is an in-pool quantity; evaluation against held-out adversaries is future work (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).
\paragraph{Per-opponent exploit averaging.} When the exploit pool contains multiple adversaries, pooled averaging lets strong performance against one mask weakness against another. We compute exploitability separately per opponent and then average, ensuring every adversary contributes equally to $e(\pi)$.
\subsection{Policy Gradient Loss}
\label{sec:pg_loss}
\paragraph{Per-round advantage normalization.}
Rather than assigning a single advantage to an entire episode, we normalize advantages \emph{per game round} across the $n$ rollouts for a given training opponent. For round $t$ and rollout $r$:
\begin{align}
\text{reward}_{t,r} &= u_{t,r} - \underbrace{(\lambda_e e_r + \lambda_c c_r + \lambda_x x_r)}_{P_r:\ \text{SEPO penalty, per-rollout}} \label{eq:round_reward}\\
A_{t,r} &= \frac{\text{reward}_{t,r} - \overline{\text{reward}}_{t}}{\sigma_t + \delta} \label{eq:advantage}
\end{align}
where $\overline{\text{reward}}_t$ and $\sigma_t$ are the mean and standard deviation across the group's rollouts at round $t$, and $\delta = 10^{-8}$ is a numerical stabilizer (distinct from the PPO clip parameter $\epsilon = 0.2$). Round-level normalization ensures that even a single divergent action at one step of one rollout produces a non-zero advantage signal, which is critical when the SFT model is near-deterministic and all rollouts produce identical episode-level outcomes. Within each group, $P_r$ varies across rollouts because each episode's own actions determine its penalty (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}), so the penalty survives normalization.
\paragraph{Group normalization erases inter-pool weighting.} Per-group advantage normalization has a consequence for multi-objective training that we believe has not been previously noted: because every group's advantages are standardized to unit variance, the $\lambda$ coefficients shape rewards only \emph{within} a group; \emph{across} groups, every pool pushes on the policy with equal force regardless of its $\lambda$. In IPD, two exploiter groups (plus the payoff signal from a stochastic training opponent) all favor defection, against a single collusive group opposing it---and under continued training the policy caves in the majority direction, driving the collusion collapse documented in Table~\ref{tab:ipd}. This implies that late-stage degeneration cannot be fixed by re-weighting $\lambda$ alone; it requires weighting groups after normalization, early stopping on $\Jsepo$ (our checkpoint rule, \S\ref{sec:setup}), or asymmetric pool sizes. We flag this as a general caution for GRPO-style algorithms with multi-term objectives.
\paragraph{PPO-style clipped surrogate loss \citep{schulman2017proximal}.} Given advantages $A_{t,r}$, the per-step policy gradient loss is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pgloss}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PG}} = -\min\!\bigl(&\rho_{t,r} A_{t,r},\\
  &\mathrm{clip}(\rho_{t,r}, 1{-}\epsilon, 1{+}\epsilon)\cdot A_{t,r}\bigr)
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{t,r} = \exp(\log\pi_\theta(a_{t,r}|s_{t,r}) - \log\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_{t,r}|s_{t,r}))$ is the importance ratio and $\epsilon = 0.2$. The KL penalty is one-sided to prevent premature convergence:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kl}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}} = \max\!\bigl(0,\; \log\pi_\theta(a|s) - \log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a|s)\bigr)
\end{equation}
The full per-step loss is $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PG}} + \beta \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}$, normalized by $|\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{train}}| \times n \times T$ and backpropagated immediately (one computation graph per step, no accumulation, keeping peak VRAM constant).
\paragraph{Memory-efficient log-softmax.} Gemma 4's vocabulary size is 262,144. Computing $\log\pi_\theta$ over the full vocabulary for a 512-token generation requires $262144 \times 512 \times 4\,\text{B} \approx 536\,\text{MB}$ per forward pass. We process generated tokens in chunks of 32, reducing peak VRAM by $16\times$ without affecting numerical results.
\paragraph{LoRA reference model.} In LoRA mode, the reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is obtained by disabling the LoRA adapters in-place (via \texttt{model.disable\_adapter()}) rather than loading a separate frozen copy. This saves ${\sim}10\,\text{GB}$ VRAM, making the full pipeline runnable on a single 46\,GB GPU.
\subsection{Action Generation and Parse Recovery}
\label{sec:parse}
\paragraph{Stopping criteria.} We implement a custom \texttt{StoppingCriteria} that halts generation when a valid action token appears on the last non-empty line of the output. The criteria respects chain-of-thought: if the output contains an open \texttt{<think>} tag without a closing \texttt{</think>}, generation continues regardless of what appears in the reasoning trace.
\paragraph{Constrained fallback decode.} When the model's free-form generation does not contain a parseable action (parse failures are rare: single-digit counts per evaluation run, varying by game and model), we recover a valid action without regex or heuristics via forced next-token decoding:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item Append the generated reasoning as an assistant turn.
  \item Add a short elicitation user turn: \textit{``State your final action (\textsc{action}/\textsc{vocab}):''}.
  \item Forward-pass the full prompt (no generation); extract logits at the final position.
  \item Set all logits to $-\infty$ except the first sub-word tokens of each valid action string.
  \item Return the argmax, which is always a valid action; no fallback is needed.
\end{enumerate}
This procedure requires one additional forward pass (no beam search, no sampling) and preserves the model's reasoning context when determining the action.
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\sepo{} Training Step}
\label{alg:sepo_grpo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\pi_\theta$, $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$, game $G$, pools $\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{tr}}, \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{ex}}, \mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{co}}$, weights $\lambda_e, \lambda_c, \lambda_x$, $\beta, \epsilon, n$
\State $\mathcal{G} \leftarrow \{(\text{pool}, o)\}$ for every opponent $o$ in every pool \Comment{$|\mathcal{G}|$ groups}
\For{each group $(p, o) \in \mathcal{G}$}
  \For{rollout $r = 1 \ldots n$}
    \State Sample episode $\tau_r$ against $o$; record $\{u_{t,r}\}_{t=1}^T$, $\log\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$
    \State $(e_r, c_r, x_r) \leftarrow$ \sepo{} metrics of $\tau_r$ alone
    \State $P_r \leftarrow \lambda_e s\, e_r + \lambda_c c_r + \lambda_x x_r$ \Comment{episode's own penalty}
  \EndFor
  \For{round $t = 1 \ldots T$}
    \State $\text{rew}_{t,r} \leftarrow s\, u_{t,r}\mathbb{1}[p{=}\text{tr}] - P_r$ for each rollout $r$
    \State $A_{t,r} \leftarrow (\text{rew}_{t,r} - \overline{\text{rew}}_t) / (\sigma_t + \delta)$ \Comment{cf.\ Eq.~\ref{eq:advantage}}
    \For{rollout $r = 1 \ldots n$}
      \State $\rho \leftarrow \exp(\log\pi_\theta(a_{t,r}|s_{t,r}) - \log\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_{t,r}|s_{t,r}))$
      \State $\mathcal{L} \leftarrow -\min(\rho A_{t,r},\, \mathrm{clip}(\rho, 1{-}\epsilon, 1{+}\epsilon) A_{t,r})$
      \State $\mathcal{L} \mathrel{+}= \beta \cdot \max(0, \log\pi_\theta - \log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$
      \State Backpropagate $\mathcal{L} / (|\mathcal{G}| \cdot n \cdot T)$
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\subsection{Training Procedure}
\label{sec:training}
Training proceeds in two stages (Appendix~E).
\paragraph{Stage 1: SFT warm-start.} We fine-tune the base model on ${\sim}44{,}000$ chain-of-thought episodes across the five games (dataset breakdown in Appendix~H, supplementary material) generated by rolling out a curated library of domain-expert strategies (TFT, NashApprox, FairSplit, etc.; mixture weights in Appendix~A). Each episode contains the full game history and an action token; reasoning traces are included. Training uses LoRA (rank 32, $\alpha = 64$, dropout 0.05, cosine LR schedule with 5\% warmup, $\eta = 2 \times 10^{-5}$, 3 epochs). Both Gemma 4 E4B-it and Qwen~3.5-4B are multimodal architectures. For text-only fine-tuning, we scope LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} targets to the \texttt{nn.Linear} attention projections (\texttt{q/k/v/o\_proj}) inside the language model submodule only, automatically excluding non-\texttt{nn.Linear} vision/audio wrappers (e.g., \texttt{Gemma4ClippableLinear} in Gemma 4). Gemma 4 additionally requires injecting \texttt{token\_type\_ids=0} to suppress the vision-tower computation path during SFT.
\paragraph{Stage 2: \sepo{}.} Starting from the merged SFT checkpoint, we run Algorithm~\ref{alg:sepo_grpo} with LoRA (rank 16, $\alpha = 32$). The optimizer is AdamW ($\eta = 10^{-5}$, gradient clip 1.0). We use $n = 4$ rollouts per training opponent, temperature 0.8, max 512 new tokens. The reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the SFT-merged base with LoRA adapters disabled. Kuhn Poker uses a stricter KL coefficient ($\beta = 0.2$ vs.\ $\beta = 0.1$ for other games) to prevent overshoot to degenerate betting strategies observed at late checkpoints with weaker KL regularization.
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Models.} We evaluate two model families across all five games: Gemma 4 E4B-it \citep{gemmateam2026gemma4} (Effective 4B instruction-tuned) as the primary model, and Qwen~3.5-4B \citep{qwen2026report} to assess cross-family generalization.
\paragraph{Training hyperparameters.} Learning rate $10^{-5}$ (AdamW), KL coefficient $\beta = 0.1$, PPO clip $\varepsilon = 0.2$, $n = 4$ rollouts per training opponent, temperature $0.8$, max 512 tokens. Kuhn Poker uses a lower learning rate ($3 \times 10^{-6}$) and higher KL coefficient ($\beta = 0.2$) to prevent KL drift.
\paragraph{Games.} We evaluate on five strategic domains: IPD (8-round prisoner's dilemma), Auction (6-round sealed-bid), Negotiation v1 (4-round single-issue), Negotiation v2/GTBench (4-round multi-issue with private valuations \citep{wu2024gtbench}), and Kuhn Poker \citep{kuhn1950simplified} (6-hand, 3-card). Full game rules, payoff structures, and exploit-pool strategies are in Appendix~F.

\paragraph{Metrics.} We report six metrics per condition. \textit{Pay/r}: mean payoff per round against the training pool ($\uparrow$). \textit{Exploit}: mean additional payoff that adversarial opponents earn over the agent ($\downarrow$), clipped at zero and computed per opponent then averaged. \textit{Coll}: collusion rate against the trusting-partner pool ($\downarrow$; e.g., defection rate against AlwaysCooperate in IPD). \textit{Ext}: domain-specific externality cost ($\downarrow$). \textit{Safety}: $\Jsepo$ evaluated at each game's $\lambda$ values with the cross-game scale $s$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:reward} ($\uparrow$). \textit{NRA} (Normalized Relative Advantage): the GTBench competitive benchmark metric, computed as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nra}
\text{NRA} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{O}|}\sum_{o \in \mathcal{O}} \frac{\sum_e \text{pay}_e^{\mathrm{LLM}} - \sum_e \text{pay}_e^{o}}{\sum_e \text{pay}_e^{\mathrm{LLM}} + \sum_e \text{pay}_e^{o}}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{O}$ is the set of all opponents and $e$ indexes episodes. NRA $\in [-1, +1]$; positive values indicate the agent outperforms its opponents in aggregate. NRA is directly comparable to \citet{wu2024gtbench} baselines for IPD and Negotiation v2.
\paragraph{Stochastic evaluation protocol.} All evaluations sample at temperature 0.8 (the training temperature) with 16 episodes per opponent and max 512 new tokens (matching training, so that models that commit to actions late are not disproportionately routed through the constrained fallback decode; per-condition fallback rates are reported in Appendix~G). We evaluate the sampled policy rather than greedy decoding for two reasons. First, greedy evaluation measures only the mode of the policy: we observed RL runs that substantially moved the sampled action distribution (training-time KL up to 1.5 from the SFT prior) without flipping a single greedy argmax along evaluation trajectories, making greedy metrics blind to the learning being measured. Second, a deterministic policy cannot represent a mixed strategy at all, and the Nash equilibria of Kuhn Poker are strictly mixed; a greedy protocol is structurally unable to exhibit the equilibrium behavior the Kuhn experiments test for. For Kuhn Poker we additionally report card-conditioned action frequencies (bluff and call rates per card) against the analytic Nash benchmark.
\paragraph{Checkpoint selection.} Checkpoints are saved every 25 steps and all are evaluated; tables report the checkpoint with the best Safety ($\Jsepo$ at the game's training $\lambda$ values), selected by this rule uniformly across all conditions and stated alongside each result. Full per-checkpoint trajectories appear in the tables or Appendix~B, so selection effects are inspectable.
\paragraph{Baselines.} For each game we report Base (off-the-shelf model, no fine-tuning), SFT (Stage 1 warm-start only, no \sepo{}), and \sepo{} (full pipeline at the best checkpoint). All reported metrics are means over 20 episodes per opponent; standard errors for payoff metrics are typically $< 5\%$ of the mean (reflecting deterministic game mechanics), and standard errors for exploit are in the range 0.02--0.05. Tables omit per-cell variance for clarity.
\paragraph{Reproducibility.} Code and SFT datasets will be released upon publication under the MIT License. Training requires a single 46\,GB GPU; wall-clock time is ${\sim}1.5$--$10.5$\,h (SFT) and ${\sim}6$--$22$\,h (\sepo{}, 100 steps) depending on game and model. Seeds: 42 (game sampling), 0 (generation). No hyperparameter search; values follow \citet{shao2024deepseekmath} defaults with per-domain KL adjustments (\S\ref{sec:training}).

\paragraph{Cross-family check (Qwen 3.5-4B).} Qwen's starting point is qualitatively weaker: its base plays a nearly card-\emph{unconditioned} strategy (bet rates $0.34/0.45/0.29$ for J/Q/K; call-K only $0.06$, a dominated fold) and requires the constrained fallback decode on ${\sim}25\%$ of decisions; its SFT dampens aggression without inducing card-conditioning. Under the corrected pipeline Qwen's \sepo{} run shows no significant frequency movement by step 25; its full trajectory was still training at submission time and is reported in Appendix~B as far as available. To test whether this is an undertraining artifact, we retrained Qwen's Kuhn SFT with doubled epochs: card-conditioning still does not emerge (bet rates $0.29/0.29/0.27$ for J/Q/K). We therefore report a boundary condition rather than a null result: \emph{SFT that teaches the game's basic structure is a precondition for equilibrium convergence under \sepo{}}. Gemma's SFT installs correct card-conditioned play and \sepo{} then adjusts the mixture toward equilibrium; when SFT fails to install the structure at all, the RL stage must learn representation and equilibrium simultaneously, which does not occur at 4B scale within our step budget.
\section{Mechanism: Where the Penalty Gradient Comes From}
\label{sec:mechanism}
Our central implementation claim---that the \sepo{} penalties must be credited to the episodes that produce them (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward})---is supported by a four-arm comparison in which the arms differ \emph{only} in the penalty's gradient path. All arms share the SFT initialization, hyperparameters, opponent pools, and evaluation protocol.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2em,noitemsep]
\item \textbf{Shared penalty (original pipeline).} One penalty estimate per opponent group (cached between refreshes). A group constant cancels identically under advantage normalization; the run's training logs confirm the penalty was byte-identical across every rollout group. This arm is \emph{exactly} payoff-only GRPO: $\lambda_e, \lambda_c, \lambda_x$ touch no gradient.
\item \textbf{Per-rollout auxiliary penalty.} Fresh exploiter/collusive episodes per rollout, credited to the training episode's log-probabilities. As shown in \S\ref{sec:grpo}, the expected penalty gradient is zero; the penalty acts as advantage noise. Empirically this arm drifts to the degenerate all-defect policy in IPD (training-time collusion $\to 1.0$ by step 40): the surviving payoff signal alone shapes behavior.
\item \textbf{Credited per-pool penalty (\sepo{}).} Eq.~\ref{eq:reward}. The only arm whose training-time collusion \emph{falls} while exploit falls (0.94 $\to$ 0.25 by step 75 in IPD training rollouts), i.e., the only arm that resists the trusting-partner exploitation that the payoff signal alone induces.
\item \textbf{Reward-only reference (re-evaluated original checkpoints).} The original-pipeline checkpoints for both model families, re-evaluated under the current protocol, serve as measured reward-only baselines (Appendix~D).
\end{enumerate}
\paragraph{What separates and what does not.} Two honest observations sharpen the claim. First, on IPD the best-checkpoint \emph{exploit} improvement over SFT is similar across arms 1--3 (${\approx}0.43 \to 0.31$): retaliation against defectors is learnable from the payoff signal alone (the stochastic mixed-strategy training opponent rewards it), consistent with the concern that exploit gains might not require the penalty terms at all. Where the arms separate is in \emph{collusion and welfare}: arms without a live collusion gradient exploit the trusting partner (arm 2 reaches collusion 1.0; \sepo{} holds 0.0 at its selected checkpoint). Second, the cross-family Kuhn comparison provides the cleanest behavioral separation available under identical evaluation: the credited pipeline moves Gemma's bluff rate monotonically onto the Nash mixture (Table~\ref{tab:kuhn}), while the original pipeline's Qwen checkpoints execute a random walk (exploit oscillating $0.29$--$0.52$ across checkpoints with no emergence of card-conditioning; Appendix~D).
\paragraph{Provenance.} We document this comparison in part because we lived its failure mode: the gradient-cancellation defect was diagnosed and fixed during development, then silently reintroduced by a merge-conflict resolution four days later, and every originally-reported run executed the reverted code. The defect is invisible in training curves (payoff-driven learning produces plausible dynamics) and invisible in evaluations (which measure the resulting policy, not the objective that trained it); it is visible only in the penalty's per-rollout variance, which our pipeline now logs explicitly at every step. Appendix~E gives the full timeline and the log signatures by which the executed code path can be identified post hoc. We release both pipelines' logs so the comparison is independently checkable.
\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
Three conditions govern when \sepo{} helps, each illustrated by one domain. (a)~\emph{A learnable equilibrium must exist}: in Auction, resisting AggressiveBid structurally conflicts with training-pool payoff, and \sepo{}'s gains are confined to utility and externality. (b)~\emph{SFT must install the game's basic structure}: in Kuhn Poker, Gemma's SFT provides card-conditioned play that \sepo{} then tunes onto the Nash mixture, while Qwen's SFT never installs card-conditioning (even with doubled training) and no equilibrium emerges within budget. (c)~\emph{Training must stop before group-normalization erases the pool weighting} (\S\ref{sec:pg_loss}): the IPD trajectory shows \sepo{}'s best checkpoint at step 25 and a collusion collapse thereafter; our $\Jsepo$-based checkpoint rule operationalizes this. On SFT regressions: under the stochastic protocol the regression is domain-dependent rather than universal---dramatic on Negotiation v2 ($17\times$), absent on IPD and Auction---which revises our earlier across-the-board claim. Where the regression exists, \sepo{} partially corrects it.
\paragraph{Comparison to GTBench.} Our NRA values use the same definition as \citet{wu2024gtbench}. Under the corrected pipeline, no negotiation configuration attains positive NRA (best: $-0.20$, \sepo{} step 50); we retract the earlier positive-NRA claim, which was an artifact of the original pipeline and checkpoint routing. Note also that in IPD, NRA is anti-correlated with safety across \sepo{} checkpoints (Table~\ref{tab:ipd}: NRA rises to $+0.43$ exactly as the policy degenerates to exploiting cooperators)---a caution against optimizing or selecting on competitive benchmark metrics in mixed-motive games.
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\sepo{} shows that objective design shapes which equilibria language agents reach---and, just as centrally, that \emph{the gradient path of the objective determines whether it trains at all}. Credited per-pool penalties move a 4B model's Kuhn Poker bluff rate monotonically onto the Nash mixture and hold collusion at zero while reducing exploitability in IPD; the same objective with a shared or auxiliary-credited penalty provably reduces to reward-only RL, whose policies drift toward exploiting trusting partners. Along the way we identify a general hazard for multi-objective GRPO (group normalization erases inter-pool weighting), establish stochastic evaluation as a requirement for measuring mixed-strategy convergence, and document---with released logs---how a silently-reverted fix caused our original results to be produced by an objective other than the one we designed. We offer the corrected pipeline, the four-arm mechanism comparison, and the honest domain-by-domain boundary conditions as the paper's contribution.
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\sepo{} has six limitations. \textbf{Single seed}: all training runs use one seed; the pipeline now supports seeded replication, but multi-seed error bars are left to future work, and we deliberately avoid bolding differences within plausible seed noise. \textbf{In-pool exploitability}: exploit is measured against a fixed adversary pool that partially overlaps the training pool in some games; held-out adversary evaluation is future work, and in Kuhn Poker we mitigate this by evaluating action frequencies against the analytic equilibrium rather than pool advantage alone. \textbf{Cross-family coverage}: under the corrected pipeline, Qwen results cover Kuhn Poker only (where they establish the SFT-precondition boundary rather than confirming convergence); Qwen's remaining domains were beyond our compute budget and their original-pipeline numbers appear only in Appendix~G. \textbf{Scope}: all games are two-player with discrete action vocabularies; $n$-player settings require coalition-aware collusion estimation. \textbf{Late-training degeneration}: continued training past the $\Jsepo$-selected checkpoint degrades collusion and welfare (\S\ref{sec:pg_loss}); \sepo{} currently manages this by early stopping rather than by construction. \textbf{Structural limits}: \sepo{} cannot reduce exploit-pool advantage where doing so structurally conflicts with training-pool payoff (Auction).
\section*{Ethical Considerations}
\sepo{} trains language agents to resist exploitation and avoid collusion in strategic economic games. First, the exploit pool design teaches agents to resist specific adversarial strategies, which a bad actor could reinterpret as a recipe for building stronger adversaries; we will publish the adversary designs to enable defensive research. Second, deploying \sepo{}-trained agents in real markets could reduce social welfare if the exploit pool under-represents human adversaries, so we recommend human-participant evaluation before deployment.
No human subjects were involved; all interactions are between computational agents, and the SFT data contain only agent-generated game histories and action tokens. A large language model assisted with \LaTeX{} formatting and editing; all content and conclusions are the authors' own.
